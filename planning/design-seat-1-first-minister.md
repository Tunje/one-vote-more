# Seat 1: First Minister (you lead the government)

Wilma's proposal for the user, 2026-11-16. Nothing in it is decided or built unless it says "today".
Companion files: `design-seat-2-cabinet-partner.md`, `design-seat-3-parliament.md`.

## What holds in every seat (the user's words)

- "you allways lead your party, but your either the leadee in the cabinet or in parliament"
- "If your party fails to take a seat that's the fail condition"
- "you can still lose your party that's another game over"

So there are three seats and two ways to lose. You move between the seats when a government forms or falls.

## When you sit here

Your party heads the government: the assembly did not vote your candidacy down (today: a First Minister is approved
unless 76 members vote against), alone or with partners or with outside parties that promised their support.

## What you do (today, for the most part)

- **Decide.** Everything the government decides comes to you or to your ministers: the budget and the laws you put to
  the assembly, what the state builds, grants, answers to unions, the charter and the home world.
- **Choose how much you decide yourself.** With delegation on, each kind of decision is set at the start of a term:
  the minister makes the call, the minister suggests and you confirm, or it comes to you open.
- **Spend your office hours.** About 40 a month. Reading, calls, meetings and decisions cost hours; what you do not
  get to is decided by a minister or left.

## What is missing here (my proposals)

1. **Your partners are people, not just votes.** A partner party holds ministries. Its leader should be able to
   argue against you in cabinet, hold a decision in their own ministries, and threaten to leave. Today partners
   are seats in a count.
2. **What you promised when the government formed is on your desk.** The agreement that got you approved is a
   list of things owed to partners and to outside supporters, with deadlines. Breaking one costs you that support.
   (Today the supporting parties have a price and a deadline, but you never see it as something to manage.)
3. **Your own party watches what you give away.** Every concession to a partner costs you standing with the part
   of your party that wanted the opposite. Enough of that and a challenger appears (see "How you lose").
4. **The opposition does something to you.** Questions you must answer or dodge, inquiries, a confidence vote
   when you look weak. Today the computer opposition is quiet between elections.

## What lands on the desk

Everything, as today: paper, reports, inbox, calls, calendar, visitors. Add two standing items:
- **The agreement**: what is owed, to whom, by when.
- **The assembly this month**: what is being asked of you, and how the votes stand.

## How you leave this seat

- **An election** you do not win, or after which no government of yours is approved: to seat 2 or seat 3.
- **A confidence vote** lost (76 against): the government falls; a new one forms, perhaps without you.
- **A partner walks out** and you no longer have the numbers.
- **You resign the government** to force an election or to go into opposition on your own terms.

## How you lose the game from here

- **Your party takes no seat** at an election.
- **Your party removes you.** Today members have ambitions, loyalty and grievances; a restless member who wants the
  leadership becomes a challenger and a contest can be held. Proposed: a contest you lose ends the game. In this
  seat the usual causes are broken promises to your own side, scandal, and a long run of bad polls.

## Open questions for you

1. May a First Minister dismiss a partner's minister, or only ask the partner's leader to?
2. Does a lost confidence vote always mean you are out, or may you try to form a new government yourself?
3. When you lose a leadership contest, is that the end at once, or is there one last chance (a second ballot, a
   party congress)?
