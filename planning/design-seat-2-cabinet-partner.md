# Seat 2: In the cabinet as a partner (you lead your party inside someone else's government)

Wilma's proposal for the user, 2026-11-16. Nothing in it is decided or built unless it says "today".
Companion files: `design-seat-1-first-minister.md`, `design-seat-3-parliament.md`.

## When you sit here

Your party is in the government and another party's leader is First Minister. Today the game forms such
governments; when it does and your party is the smaller one, you have nothing to do: the computer decides.

## The idea in one line

You run your part of the government, you argue about the rest, and your one real weapon is leaving.

## What you do

1. **Run your ministries.** The portfolios your party was given are yours. Decisions in them come to your desk
   exactly as they come to a First Minister's, and you decide them. This is the delegation the game already has,
   seen from the minister's chair: the cases a minister "makes the call" on are your cases.
2. **Argue in cabinet.** Decisions outside your ministries that are big enough to go to the whole cabinet come to
   you as a position to take: back it, oppose it, or trade (I back this if you give me that). You do not decide
   them. The First Minister counts the cabinet and decides.
3. **Hold or deliver your party's votes.** In the assembly your members vote as you say, more or less (your
   party's own discipline applies). Voting against your own government is possible and is a crisis.
4. **Keep the agreement.** When the government formed you were promised things: ministries, a policy, money for a
   cause. They are on your desk as what you are owed and what you owe. You press for yours and decide how much of
   theirs you swallow.
5. **Threaten to leave, or leave.** Walking out takes your seats away. If the government then has too few, it
   falls. A threat that is believed gets concessions; a threat made too often is ignored.

## What you cannot do

- Set the budget as a whole, sign for the government, or answer the home world. Those are the First Minister's.
- See everything. You get your ministries' reports in full and the rest as a cabinet member would: summaries, and
  what your own people hear.

## What lands on the desk

- **In-tray and inbox:** your ministries' cases and reports; cabinet papers for the month.
- **Red phone:** the First Minister, and the leaders of the other partners.
- **Door:** your own ministers and your party's members.
- **A standing item:** the agreement, with what is owed to you and by you.

## The pressure on you

- **Blame without credit.** Voters hold the whole government responsible. Your party pays for the First
  Minister's failures and gets little for the successes outside your ministries.
- **Your own party.** Every compromise you accept counts against you with the members who wanted the opposite.
  Stay too long in a government that gives you nothing and a challenger appears.
- **The First Minister can replace you as a partner** if another party offers the same seats for less.

## How you leave this seat

- **Up, to seat 1:** an election after which you lead; or the government falls and the new one is yours.
- **Down, to seat 3:** you walk out; you are dropped; the government falls and the new one is formed without you;
  an election leaves you outside.

## How you lose the game from here

- Your party takes no seat at an election (partners in unpopular governments are the ones this happens to).
- Your party removes you: the usual cause here is having given away too much.

## What exists today that this stands on

- Governments of several parties, and the agreement prices of the outside supporters.
- Delegation: decision types by portfolio, with a minister's call, a suggestion, or the First Minister's own.
- Members with ambitions and grievances, challengers, and a contest.

## What would have to be built

- Portfolios actually held by a party, and their cases routed to the player when the player holds them.
- The cabinet as a place where positions are taken and counted.
- A computer First Minister who deals with you: offers, refusals, and a judgement of whether your threat is real.
- Leaving a government as an action, and a government falling because of it.

## Open questions for you

1. How many ministries does a partner get: by its share of the government's seats, or by what it bargained for?
2. May you choose which ministries you ask for when the government forms?
3. If you vote against your own government, are you out at once, or is that the First Minister's choice?

## The user's answers, 2026-11-16 (verbatim)

On each seat having its own room: "yes each room should be different".

On how a partner gets ministries: "if your in the alliance that leads the ministry seats will be handed out too you
by the vote count, so if you party hold about half thats 4 seats, if you hold about 10 percent thats 1, and so forth
remember an alliance can be built by many parites so you might be one of a few, and you would have to argu your way
into a seat based on your comatibility if they pick other alliance partners they you go down one step, and remember
the way the primisiter can set ministers level of work autonomus, recomendations and that? that will be done for
you"

So:
- Ministries in the governing alliance are handed out by vote count: about half the alliance's votes is four
  ministries, about a tenth is one, and so on in proportion.
- An alliance can be many parties; you may be one of several partners.
- You argue your way into the alliance on your compatibility with it. If they pick other partners, you go down one
  step: to parliament.
- The First Minister sets how much your ministers decide on their own (the delegation levels: make the call,
  recommend, or the First Minister's own). In this seat that is done to you, not by you.
